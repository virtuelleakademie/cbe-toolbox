# The PIONEER-Alliance Definition of Challenge-Based Education

> **Provenance.** This document is grounded in the validated PIONEER Working
> Package 4 source *4.2.1 Sub-Task Didactical Toolbox — Train-the-Trainer*
> (§§4.1–4.3), the alliance's consolidated "common language" and "corpus of
> PIONEER-relevant CBE principles". It states what PIONEER means by CBE; it does
> not replace the Challenge Based Learning design spine (Big Idea → Essential
> Question → Challenge → Investigate → Act) documented in `methodology-001`.

## Summary

The PIONEER Alliance defines Challenge-Based Education (CBE) as an experiential, student-centred pedagogy in which learning is organised around **authentic societal challenges co-defined with external actors and aimed at creating actionable value**. CBE builds on problem- and project-based learning but adds a stronger focus on stakeholder co-creation, societal contribution, and real-world implementation. WP4 operationalises CBE through seven criteria, each with a three-stage maturity cue, and anchors the approach in a multi-actor ecosystem and the SDG11 goal of inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable cities and communities.

## What CBE is — and how it differs from PBL and PjBL

Like other experiential and student-centred pedagogies, CBE engages students in complex questions, uncertainty, and collaboration. Its distinctive feature is that learning is organised around authentic societal challenges co-defined with external actors and aimed at creating actionable value.

- **Problem-based learning (PBL)** starts from an open-ended problem that drives inquiry; its primary purpose is to support students in understanding concepts, analysing a problem situation, and developing problem-solving competences. The problem may be realistic and complex, but it does not necessarily require direct collaboration with external stakeholders or implementation beyond the classroom.
- **Project-based learning (PjBL)** is organised around an extended project through which students produce a tangible outcome — a report, prototype, presentation or artefact — often culminating in a public product. The project can be highly authentic, but it may still be primarily designed by the lecturer and situated within the educational setting rather than embedded in a wider societal innovation process.
- **CBE** goes one step further by placing the challenge itself at the centre of a multi-actor learning ecosystem. A CBE challenge is not merely a task or case; it is a real-life issue connected to societal, regional or global needs — urban transformation, mobility, inclusion, sustainability, digital transition.

In short: **CBE builds on problem- and project-based learning but adds a stronger focus on stakeholder co-creation, societal contribution, and real-world implementation.** It is further distinguished by an explicit value and impact orientation, transdisciplinarity, and an iterative learning process in which ideas are tested, challenged, revised and improved through stakeholder feedback — so reflection and evaluation are central design elements, not add-ons.

## The multi-actor ecosystem

External stakeholders — municipalities, companies, NGOs, citizens or expert communities — are **not only audiences** for student work. In CBE they can act as:

- **problem owners** — the challenge originates from a real stakeholder's need;
- **co-creators** — knowledge is co-created across academic and non-academic domains;
- **feedback partners** — stakeholders interact with students at defined moments and across iterative cycles;
- **potential users** of the results — outputs are relevant beyond assessment and can contribute to decisions or follow-up actions.

Because societal challenges rarely fit within one discipline, CBE deliberately brings together different disciplinary perspectives and, where possible, different institutional, cultural and national contexts. This makes CBE especially suitable for European university alliances, where students and lecturers from different countries work jointly with urban, economic, political and civil-society actors.

## SDG11 and the impact orientation

CBE intentionally connects learning with societal contribution. In the PIONEER context this is particularly linked to **SDG 11** and the goal of developing **inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable cities and communities**. Students are expected not only to understand a problem or deliver a project, but to develop context-sensitive insights, feasible concepts, prototypes or recommendations that can create value for stakeholders and communities.

## The seven operationalised PIONEER criteria

WP4 discussed and defined a set of CBE criteria for use across the PIONEER Alliance and operationalised them to support both the design of new educational offers and the redesign of existing programmes. Each criterion carries a **three-stage maturity cue** (a low → mid → high developmental progression). These seven criteria are the rows of the CBE maturity raster documented in `methodology-007`.

1. **Societal, real-world challenge.** An authentic societal problem that is complex and open-ended (no single "correct" answer) and relevant beyond the classroom.
   *Maturity cue:* topic referenced but closed/predefined → open-ended real issue → stakeholder-originated, open-ended, aims at societal value.
2. **External stakeholder involvement.** CBE systematically involves real-life actors (municipalities, NGOs, companies, citizen groups) as problem owners, feedback partners, and/or co-creators.
   *Maturity cue:* guest speaker/case provider → planned interactions at defined moments → stakeholder as challenge owner/co-creator throughout.
3. **Inter-/transdisciplinary collaboration.** Learners integrate multiple disciplines and, at stronger levels, co-create knowledge with non-academic stakeholders (transdisciplinarity).
   *Maturity cue:* "consider other perspectives" → explicit integration of domains → academic + non-academic co-creation.
4. **Student agency & ownership and the role of the teacher.** Students act as active agents who make meaningful decisions; teachers shift from content transmitter to facilitator/coach/co-creator.
   *Maturity cue:* limited choices within a predefined framework → students make key decisions, teacher facilitates → students own the process, teacher enables/coaches/reflects.
5. **Value-orientation and reflection.** Students reflect on ethical implications, societal values, and the impact of their solutions — ideally embedded throughout the module, not only at the end.
   *Maturity cue:* mentioned but not systematic → reflection on ethics/impact → value considerations embedded across the process.
6. **Societal impact and transferability.** Outputs are relevant beyond assessment: shared with stakeholders/communities and, in strong CBE, contribute to decisions or follow-up actions.
   *Maturity cue:* assessment-only output (not yet CBE) → relevance discussed → outputs shared → outputs contribute to real change/follow-up.
7. **Iterative learning process.** Learning progresses in cycles (research → design → test → reflect → improve), with feedback loops and experiential work. The PIONEER framing also names this as the integration of education, research, and practice.
   *Maturity cue:* some practice, little iteration → structured phases with feedback → multiple cycles of testing, feedback, reflection, adaptation.
