# CBL Implementation Scales and Entry Points

## Summary

The CBL Guide describes implementation at different scales. At the macro level, CBL can be an overarching philosophical approach for strategic planning, curriculum development, and classroom practice; at a smaller scale, it can structure bounded Challenge events such as capstones (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 14–16). This document uses **institutional-scale implementation** and **bounded Challenge implementation** as corpus framing for those two Guide-backed poles, not as official CBL terms.

## Institutional-Scale Implementation

At institutional scale, CBL informs decisions beyond one course or event:

- At the macro level it is "an overarching philosophical approach that uses a framework of Challenges to inform and guide strategic decision-making, curriculum development, and classroom practice" (CBL Guide 2016, p. 14).
- It can be "implemented as a guiding pedagogy or integrated with other progressive approaches to learning" (p. 7), including project-based learning and design-oriented approaches.
- Strategic Challenges can help an organization define its mission, identify Challenges, create a common language, and develop strategic plans (p. 16).

In this framing, CBL is an organizing approach rather than a method confined to a single class.

## Bounded Challenge Implementation

A bounded Challenge is a finite event embedded within a broader curriculum:

- **Nano Challenges** are short, tightly bounded, and teacher-directed; **Mini Challenges** use the full framework over roughly two to four weeks; **Standard Challenges** last one month or longer (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 14–15).
- The framework can extend current practice and support specific capstone events during the school year (p. 14).
- **Capstone Challenges** are Standard Challenges used as culminating academic and intellectual experiences, such as a senior project that acts as a graduation thesis (p. 15).
- A bounded Challenge may integrate prior learning or community engagement. *Practice observation: intensive weeks and hackathon-like formats are common higher-education adaptations, but the Guide does not name them.*

## Implications for CBL Tool Design

*Practice observation (corpus-author analysis; not sourced from the CBL documentation):* the two implementation scales create different needs for an assessment or facilitation tool:

| Dimension | Institutional scale | Bounded Challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Institution-wide | Course/module level |
| Duration | Ongoing, iterative | Finite event |
| Assessment integration | Formative throughout | Formative and summative within the event |
| Teacher role | Sustained co-learning and facilitation | Facilitation during the event |
| Data needs | Longitudinal, portfolio | Event-bound snapshots |

## Multiple Points of Entry

CBL "is designed to be flexible, customizable and allow for multiple points of entry" (CBL Guide 2016, p. 14) and is "a scalable model with multiple points of entry and the ability to start small and build big" (p. 7). An institution can:

1. Begin with a single capstone Challenge.
2. Embed CBL in one teacher's practice.
3. Use CBL to extend existing project-based work.
4. Adopt CBL as an overarching institutional framework over time.

The Guide presents the Challenge variations from Nano through Strategic as scaffolding for moving from individual Challenges toward school-wide implementation (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 14–16).

## Notes on Meta-Skills

> **Corpus annotation (2026-11-05):** The Guide does not compare the meta-skill effects of different implementation scales. Repeated institutional practice may create more opportunities for reflection and transfer than a single bounded event, but that is a corpus inference rather than a claim established by the Guide.
