# CBL and Its Experiential Learning Foundations

## Summary

Challenge Based Learning explicitly situates itself within a long tradition of progressive and experiential education. Understanding these roots clarifies why CBL looks the way it does and where it diverges from purely constructivist approaches.

## The Acknowledged Lineage

The CBL Guide states directly that the framework "builds on the foundation of experiential learning and leans heavily on the wisdom of a long history of progressive ideas" (CBL Guide 2016, p. 6). The guide also says that the framework is informed by ideas from education, media, technology, entertainment, recreation, the workplace, and society (p. 6).

### Key Ancestral Traditions

| Tradition | Representative Thinkers | CBL Connection |
|---|---|---|
| Experiential Learning | Dewey (1938), Kolb (1984) | Learning-by-doing, reflection cycle |
| Constructivism | Piaget, Vygotsky, Bruner | Knowledge constructed through experience |
| Progressive Education | Freire (2005), Palmer (1983) | Learner agency, critical consciousness |
| Service Learning | — | Community focus, civic action |
| Critical Pedagogy | Freire | Activism, societal challenges |

## John Dewey's Influence

Dewey's argument that education must connect to the learner's lived experience is operationalized directly in the CBL Engage phase: the Essential Questioning process is designed to ensure learners personally connect to content before research begins. The CBL foundational idea *"Learner Inspired, Learner Directed"* (CBL Guide 2016, p. 9) maps closely to Dewey's insistence that education must begin with the student's own impulses and interests.

## Kolb's Learning Cycle

Kolb's four-stage model (Concrete Experience → Reflective Observation → Abstract Conceptualization → Active Experimentation) maps onto CBL's phases with notable similarities. CBL's continuous *Document, Reflect and Share* layer (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 11, 42–43) is the direct operational equivalent of Kolb's Reflective Observation stage — except CBL makes it concurrent rather than sequential.

## Vygotsky and the Zone of Proximal Development

The CBL foundational idea *"Boundaries of Adventure"* — where boundaries guide the way and provide freedom for learners to take ownership (CBL Guide 2016, p. 9) — echoes Vygotsky's scaffolding concept. The challenge is deliberately pitched at a level that requires effort and collaboration, not at something already mastered.

## What CBL Adds to the Tradition

CBL's distinctive contribution relative to its predecessors:
1. **Authentic technology use** — not technology-for-technology's-sake but as a genuine tool for research, communication, and publication
2. **The Challenge as civic frame** — challenges are oriented toward real-world impact, not just skill acquisition
3. **Public documentation and sharing** — outputs are published for real audiences, not just graded by teachers

## Notes on Meta-Skills

> **Corpus annotation (2026-05-21):** The foundational literature cited by CBL (Dewey, Kolb, Vygotsky, Bruner) is primarily concerned with *how* learning happens, not with explicitly teaching learners *how to learn.* The meta-skill dimension — explicitly developing learners' capacity to navigate ambiguous challenges — is **not** a foregrounded concept in this lineage as presented by CBL. It is structurally present (Kolb's reflection stage, Dewey's growth orientation) but not named or taught as a meta-competency. This contrasts with frameworks like Hattie's visible learning or Zimmermann's self-regulated learning, which do make meta-cognition an explicit target.
