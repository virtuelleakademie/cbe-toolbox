# CBL Core Vocabulary: Official Terminology and Contested Concepts

## Purpose

This vocabulary document serves two functions:
1. Define the official CBL terms, anchored to where the CBL Guide 2016 defines them
2. Flag terminology gaps and contested usages relevant to tool development — especially the **meta-skills question**

The guide itself insists on this discipline: "A consistent vocabulary is critical to the success of all frameworks and plans" (CBL Guide 2016, p. 8).

---

## Official CBL Terms

### Big Idea
"A broad concept that can be explored in multiple ways", relevant to the learners and the larger community (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 11, 31–32). Big Ideas are deliberately open-ended — the guide's examples are *Community, Relationships, Creativity, Health, Sustainability, Democracy* (p. 32). They are entry points, not assignments.

*Tool implication (practice observation):* the system should support Big Idea articulation without narrowing too early. A well-formed Big Idea should be recognizable but not prescriptive.

### Essential Question
The question that bridges a Big Idea to a learner's specific context and interest. Essential Questioning "allows the Learners to contextualize and personalize the Big Idea"; the end product is "a single Essential Question that is relevant to the individual or group" (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12, 32–33).

*Tool implication (practice observation):* distinguish between questions that are genuinely essential (open, personally motivated, pointing toward a Challenge) and questions that are merely informational (answerable by lookup).

### Challenge Statement
The actionable formulation derived from the Essential Question — the Challenge "turns the Essential Questions into a call to action by charging participants to learn about the subject and develop a Solution"; Challenges are "immediate and actionable" (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12, 33–34). Well-formed Challenges are "difficult and have multiple possibilities for Solutions", neither too broad nor too narrow (p. 34).

*Distinguishing from Problem (practice observation):* a Challenge implies the learner is responsible for changing a condition. A Problem implies solving a puzzle.

### Guiding Questions
Questions generated at the start of the Investigate phase — they "include everything that needs to be learned to develop an informed Solution to the Challenge" (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12, 36). The learners consolidate similar questions, identify patterns, and create their **own** categories (in the guide's Health-Challenge example: science, exercise, education, diet), then sort and prioritize within them ("must know" at the top), producing "a set of categories that contain prioritized sets of questions" (pp. 37–38). The guide prescribes no fixed question taxonomy — the categories emerge from the learners' questions.

### Guiding Activities / Guiding Resources
The activities (simulations, experiments, projects, problem sets, research, expert interviews, surveys, lectures) and resources (online databases and journals, online courses, libraries, social networks, experts) used to answer the Guiding Questions (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12, 38).

### Analysis
The Investigate step in which learners "analyze the accumulated data and identify themes", moving "from a list of findings to research conclusions" that set the foundation for the solution (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 39–40).

### Solution Concept
A defined direction for addressing the Challenge, emerging "from the findings made during the investigation phase" — a campaign, improvement project, product, or other activity; not yet a prototype (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 13, 41).

### Implementation and Evaluation
The phase where solutions meet reality: implementation "takes place within a real setting with an authentic audience"; learners "measure outcomes, reflect on what worked and what didn't and determine their impact on the Challenge" (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 13, 41–42).

### Document, Reflect and Share
The continuous meta-process running through all phases — "Supporting the entire process is an ongoing process of documenting, reflecting and sharing" (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 11, 42–43). Not a phase itself — a discipline.

### Informative and Summative Assessment
The guide's two assessment strategies: "Informative assessment occurs continuously throughout the process and guides the learning process, while summative assessment evaluates progress at specific checkpoints or the conclusion" (CBL Guide 2016, p. 25).

## Contested and Gap Vocabulary

### "21st-Century Skills"
The CBL Guide consistently uses **21st-century skills** as its outcome term. It says learners "organically develop 21st-century skills" through authentic learning experiences and also names them in its overview of the framework's intended outcomes (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 4, 6, 9). The Guide does not enumerate those skills as an assessable list, so any such list needs a separate source.

### "Durable Skills" — NOT a guide term
> **Critical annotation (corrected 2026-07-09):** The term **durable skills** does **not** appear in the CBL Guide 2016 (phrase-level check against the full text). An earlier revision of this document claimed the 2016 guide "shifts toward durable skills" — that was wrong; the guide consistently says "21st-century skills". "Durable skills" is later ed-sector vocabulary that some CBL-adjacent materials use. When corpus or tool text needs a term for transferable competencies, prefer the guide's own "21st-century skills" when citing CBL, and mark "durable skills" as external vocabulary.

### "Meta-Skills" — NOT an official CBL term
> **Critical annotation:** The term **meta-skills** does **not appear** in the CBL Guide 2016. The Guide's vocabulary is "21st-century skills"; none of its defined terms names the capacity to *manage one's own learning process* — which is the core definition of a meta-skill.
>
> The closest official reference to a meta-level learning capacity is the guide's stated goal of "developing a framework for life-long learning" (CBL Guide 2016, p. 4). However, this is aspirational framing, not an operationalized construct.
>
> **Implication for a CBL assessment tool:** if the tool intends to assess or develop meta-skills (e.g., self-regulation, reflection strategies, transfer of challenge-solving approaches), this represents a **deliberate extension** of the official CBL framework, not something found within it. This extension may be well-justified — the CBL process is structurally well-suited to meta-skill development — but it should be named as an extension, not presented as part of the official CBL canon.

### "A Framework for Life-Long Learning"
The guide's Welcome text names "developing a framework for life-long learning" as one of the things CBL provides alongside 21st-century skills and immediate impact (CBL Guide 2016, p. 4). This is the official text's most direct reference to a meta-learning orientation. The phrase "learn how to learn" — attributed to the guide in an earlier revision of this document — does **not** occur in it. The life-long-learning goal is not elaborated into specific practices or assessable outcomes.

### "Authentic Learning"
Used throughout the guide to mean learning grounded in real-world problems with real stakeholders and genuine consequences — "authentic learning experiences foster in-depth content knowledge" (CBL Guide 2016, p. 9); solutions are implemented "with an authentic audience" (pp. 12–13, 41). Authenticity is the precondition for Challenges creating urgency and spurring action (p. 9) as intended.
