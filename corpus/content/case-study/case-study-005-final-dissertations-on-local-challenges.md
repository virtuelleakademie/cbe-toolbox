# TFGs y TFMs — Final Dissertations on Local Challenges

> **Collection note.** This project-authored case preserves the supplied module account, its distinctions and its visible gaps. The supplied office or PDF file and the assessment workbook are not bibliographic sources.

## Summary

Undergraduate and master’s dissertations addressing real-world socio-economic and socio-territorial challenges, directed and supervised by academic staff within the departments of Applied Economics and Social Work and Social Services at the University of Huelva.

## Module Identity

### Module Title

TFGs y TFMs (UHU_TFE)

### Short Description

Undergraduate and master’s dissertations addressing real-world socio-economic and socio-territorial challenges, directed and supervised by academic staff within the departments of Applied Economics and Social Work and Social Services at the University of Huelva.

### University / Institution

University of Huelva

### Program Level

BA / MA /

It could be any of them.

### Program / Discipline

Social Work /Economy

BA Social Work / BA Bachelor’s Degree in Industrial Relations and Human Resources

MA “Máster oficial interuniversitario en empleo Estrategias y gestión de servicios y políticas territoriales de Empleo”( Official inter-university Master's Degree in Employment: Strategies and Management of Territorial Employment Services and Policies) /MA “Máster official en investigación e intervención en Trabajo Social”( Official Master's Degree in Research and Intervention in Social Work)

### ECTS Credits

6

### Duration

Second term of the academic year. 150 hours.

### Cohort Size

Between 2 and 5 students. Students take part on an individual basis, as they link their final-year project to specific challenges.

## Delivery Architecture

### Delivery Form

This is a specialised programme within the framework of the final-year projects (TFG or TFM) for the relevant BA or MA degrees. It involves tutoring students throughout the semester as they tackle specific real-world challenges related to their field of study.

### Delivery Format

Final written dissertation and oral defence. In accordance with the formal guidelines for each degree programme.

### Institutional Constraints

This is an initiative undertaken both individually and as a team by the tutors, lecturers and researchers involved. There is as yet no formal framework for developing these modules in relation to specific, real-world challenges facing the region. Work is carried out either individually or in pairs of students from different disciplines (though not always) on the same challenge.

There is NO extra credit for this more active participation; it is assessed in the standard way, just like the rest of the students’ work.

### Design Constraints

This initiative must be carried out in accordance with the teaching guidelines for these Bachelor’s and Master’s degree modules, and must therefore adhere to the established system of coursework, tutorials and assessment weightings. The work must comply with the formal requirements specified for each degree programme. There is no provision for collaborative work or group work. The final process and assessment are individual.

## Challenge & Stakeholders

### Challenge Topic

Work has been carried out on current social, economic, socio-ecological and territorial challenges, with links to the SDGs, the Urban Agenda and broader global frameworks. These are local challenges; for example, work has been done on: sustainable tourism, local entrepreneurship, community-led urban agendas, and the assessment of older people’s needs through citizen science in neighbourhoods facing social exclusion, amongst others.

### Challenge Formulation

The challenges have been shaped by specific projects currently underway, as well as by the students’ engagement with the local area and various local stakeholders.

### Challenge Authenticity / Origin

The analysis focuses on current global issues, but above all on those linked to local realities.

### Challenge Owner / Stakeholders

These are issues that affect everyone in different ways. It is usually at a local level and involves specific stakeholders or established networks of stakeholders.

### Stakeholder Involvement Moments

Students and teachers connect from the very start of the course. The project stems from the students’ choice of topic, with guidance from the participating teachers. The opportunity to connect through the teachers’ formal and informal networks presents students with achievable challenges.

The stakeholders are then brought together with the students to create shared opportunities for understanding the challenge, participating in group activities and, where appropriate, conducting research on specific projects. The students’ research and proposals are discussed with the stakeholders to identify areas for adjustment or improvement, as well as ways to implement or develop them.

## Constructive Alignment

### Intended Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module, students will be able to:

Identify and analyse real-world challenges of social, economic, socio-ecological and territorial nature, connecting local realities to global frameworks such as the SDGs and the Urban Agenda.

Apply disciplinary knowledge from their field of study (Social Work, Economics, Industrial Relations, etc.) to address authentic, complex challenges with practical and societal relevance.

Conduct rigorous and in-depth research using a variety of methodologies — including interviews, focus groups, co-diagnosis and co-design workshops — in a professional and ethical manner.

Engage professionally with stakeholders and external partners, demonstrating effective communication, active listening, and the capacity to negotiate and co-create solutions in real-world settings.

Develop and present a well-grounded project proposal that responds to a specific challenge, meeting the formal academic requirements of their degree programme while remaining meaningful and applicable to the stakeholders involved.

Exercise critical and creative thinking to move from problem understanding to the design of feasible, evidence-based proposals or solutions.

Work collaboratively across disciplines, when applicable, drawing on diverse perspectives to enrich the analysis and response to the challenge.

Demonstrate commitment to sustainable development and the public good, integrating values of social responsibility, territorial equity and civic engagement into their academic and professional practice.

Reflect on their own learning process, developing self-awareness about their strengths, limitations and growth as emerging professionals.

### Key Learning Activities

Relevant reading material related to the challenge

Understanding of the stakeholders, the process and the challenge

A professional approach to the stakeholders and the situation

Consultation with professionals involved in the challenge

Proposal for a final-year undergraduate or postgraduate project. Development and feedback with the relevant stakeholders.

Formal drafting of the project and presentation, tailored to the practical realities of the challenge being addressed.

### Assessment Criteria / Rubrics

This depends on the course syllabuses for each associated degree programme, which are available on the University of Huelva’s official websites. Tutors do receive a positive assessment for their part of the assessment process for participating in and engaging with an innovative, challenge-based approach to learning.

### Assessment Evidence & Feedback

Students play an active role in the follow-up and mentoring sessions.

Throughout the sessions, they engage with the stakeholders and work collaboratively with teachers, students and professionals, in a supportive and mentoring capacity. This is well received by the students, who find it enriching and practical

## Phase-by-Phase Walkthrough

### Phase 1 – Engage

Students are encouraged to consider this learning methodology as a potential approach for their undergraduate and postgraduate dissertations, given that it addresses a topic of interest linked to real-world challenges. Information and relevant documentation are provided to them.

They are also shown examples of work by fellow students and given information about the stakeholders involved.

### Phase 2 – Investigate

They engage with the local context surrounding the challenge, with the relevant stakeholders and professionals linked to the teaching staff involved. This leads to the research phase on the challenge, conducted both informally and formally using a variety of methodologies (interviews, focus groups, co-diagnosis and co-design workshops, etc.).

### Phase 3 – Act / Prototype

Throughout the semester’s tutorials and meetings with stakeholders, students move from listening and exchanging ideas to forging partnerships and proposing their final project.

Their ideas are presented to the stakeholders and teaching staff; they are initially refined, and work begins on the solution or prototype project for the challenge.

### Phase 4 – Reflect & Present

The assessment takes place in two stages. There is an assessment of the candidates themselves and another stage involving the formal evaluation of their work by qualified examiners.

## Instructor Perspective

### Preparation Required

It has been, and remains, essential for teaching staff to engage with local stakeholders and diverse networks that enable students’ proposals to be linked to real-world situations and known challenges. The preparatory work also includes establishing initial contact with stakeholders to propose involving a student in specific challenges. This involves ongoing three-way support.

It also requires an initial approach, as well as the development and collection of materials on the challenge, to position the students in the first phase.

### Instructor Role During Module

Teaching staff are responsible for monitoring pupils’ progress, offering guidance when difficulties arise, answering their questions and encouraging their participation. They must also be equally available to those involved in the various challenges.

### Challenges & Tips

The main challenge lies in engaging students in an experience that places heavy demands on skills they have not yet fully developed, such as research, critical thinking and openness to new ways of learning. It is also difficult to introduce other teachers to the approach so that they can implement it, as it relies heavily on the teachers’ own connections with stakeholders.

The rigidity of the assessment also means that students are sometimes not motivated to participate.

Recommendations from students in different year groups help to raise awareness and understanding of this option.

## Outcomes & Transferability

### Student Experience

> Practice observation, not empirically grounded. The following account is retained from the supplied module template; no independent outcome evidence is added.

Students who have participated in this approach to their undergraduate or postgraduate dissertation report a highly enriching and meaningful learning experience. Engaging with real-world challenges connected to their local community gives them a strong sense of purpose and relevance in their academic work. They value the opportunity to interact directly with stakeholders and professionals, which broadens their perspective and develops practical competencies beyond what traditional coursework offers.

Students highlight the development of critical thinking, research skills and professional communication as key outcomes of the experience. Working alongside teachers, peers from other disciplines and external stakeholders in a mentoring and collaborative setting is seen as particularly valuable. The interdisciplinary nature of some projects, when students from Social Work and Economics work on the same challenge, is noted as a source of personal and academic growth. Although the formal assessment framework remains the same as for conventional dissertations, students generally find the process more motivating and professionally relevant, and many report that the experience has a direct impact on their career orientation and personal commitment to social and territorial challenges.

### Transferability

This model has strong potential for transferability to other universities and disciplinary contexts, as it builds on an existing and widely recognised academic structure — the final-year undergraduate and postgraduate dissertation — without requiring significant institutional change or additional resources. The key conditions for replication are the availability of teaching staff who are willing to engage with local stakeholder networks and to act as facilitators between students and real-world challenges.

The approach is particularly well suited to disciplines that address social, economic, territorial or environmental issues, such as Social Work, Economics, Education, Urban Studies or Environmental Sciences, though it can be adapted to any field where real-world application and community engagement are relevant. It is equally applicable at both Bachelor’s and Master’s level. The challenge-based framing aligns naturally with global frameworks such as the SDGs and the Urban Agenda, which further supports its adoption in international academic contexts. One critical success factor for transferability is the progressive development of a network of local and regional stakeholders who are willing to co-define challenges and engage with students throughout the process. Formal institutional recognition of this type of active, challenge-based tutoring — for example, through dedicated credits, workload acknowledgement or accreditation schemes — would significantly enhance scalability and sustainability across institutions.
