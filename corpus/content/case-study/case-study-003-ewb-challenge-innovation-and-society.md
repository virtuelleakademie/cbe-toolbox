# Innovation & Society: Engineers Without Borders Challenge

> **Collection note.** This project-authored case preserves the supplied module account, its distinctions and its visible gaps. The supplied office or PDF file and the assessment workbook are not bibliographic sources.

## Summary

This master-level module uses the Engineers Without Borders Challenge as an authentic Challenge-Based Education setting in which students develop context-sensitive, sustainability-oriented engineering concepts for real-world socio-technical problems. Students work in teams, apply innovation and design-thinking methods, engage with external stakeholder perspectives, prototype solutions, and reflect on both product and process learning.

## Delivery Architecture

### Delivery Form

On-site with hybrid/online stakeholder elements

### Delivery Format

Semester module; Challenge-Based Learning / Challenge-Based Education; real-world NGO challenge; team-based innovation project; method labs; interim feedback; final presentation and documentation.

### Institutional Constraints

The module is embedded as a mandatory 5 ECTS course in the master curricula of Mechanical Engineering – Smart Systems and Green Building Engineering. The semester structure requires the challenge process to be completed within one teaching term, including onboarding, method training, independent teamwork, feedback, final presentation, documentation, and reflection.

### Design Constraints

The challenge design depends on the annual Engineers Without Borders partner brief, availability of partner and expert feedback, students’ disciplinary backgrounds, and the financial feasibility of prototyping within one semester. Because the challenges are authentic, requirements may remain partly ambiguous; this ambiguity is a deliberate learning condition but requires scaffolding, coaching, reflection, and clear process milestones.

## Challenge & Stakeholders

### Challenge Topic

Responsible engineering for sustainability-oriented development cooperation: context-sensitive, low-tech, locally adaptable engineering solutions for communities in the Global South. Topics change each year according to current needs of the development cooperation partners.

### Challenge Formulation

The exact formulation changes by cohort. In 2024/25, students developed context-sensitive sanitation concepts for rural households in Sierra Leone, adapting urine-diverting dry toilet systems to domestic contexts while considering local resources, spatial constraints, cultural practices, and educational accessibility. In winter semester 2025/26, students designed a universal, modular water-storage system for wind-driven water pumps that could be adapted locally, built and maintained with available tools and materials, and operated without electronic components.

### Challenge Authenticity / Origin

The challenges are sourced from real Engineers Without Borders Germany contexts and partner organisations. The 2024/25 challenge was linked to Engineers Without Borders Germany and MADAM in Sierra Leone; the 2025/26 challenge was identified with Green Desert e.V., the Open Windmill Initiative, and Engineers Without Borders Germany.

### Challenge Owner / Stakeholders

Primary stakeholders include Engineers Without Borders Germany, partner NGOs, local communities affected by the challenge, external experts and practitioners, students, teaching staff, and the university. Depending on the annual brief, community needs, local resources, maintenance capacities, and cultural practices become central stakeholder-related design criteria.

### Stakeholder Involvement Moments

Students receive practical insights from external stakeholders and practitioners at the beginning of the semester. During the challenge, stakeholder perspectives enter through the challenge brief, fact sheets and background materials, virtual expert or partner consultations where available, interim feedback, and final presentation and discussion formats. Student statements describe virtual consultations with partner organisations and local experts as important for feasibility checking.

## Constructive Alignment

### Intended Learning Outcomes

After completing the module, students will be able to:

- Analyse open-ended socio-technical engineering challenges in relation to local, cultural, ecological, and resource-related constraints.

- Apply innovation-management and design-thinking methods, including user-centred design, ideation, and rapid prototyping, to develop feasible solution concepts.

- Develop and justify context-sensitive, sustainability-oriented engineering concepts under uncertainty and incomplete, brittle information.

- Collaborate in interdisciplinary teams and communicate design decisions, assumptions, trade-offs, and limitations.

- Reflect critically on their own learning, teamwork, communication, ambiguity tolerance, and responsibility as future engineers.

### Key Learning Activities

Introductory sessions on innovation processes and sustainability-oriented engineering; stakeholder and practitioner dialogue; structured method labs on user-centred design, ideation, creativity techniques, and rapid prototyping; independent teamwork; iterative concept development and prototyping; interim presentations; reflection sessions; final presentation; technical documentation; reflective reports.

### Assessment Criteria / Rubrics

Contextual fit, stakeholder and user orientation, sustainability and local adaptability, technical feasibility, quality of innovation process, evidence-based justification, prototype or concept quality, communication quality, team collaboration, and depth of reflection.

### Assessment Evidence & Feedback

Students submit or present a developed concept or prototype, a final presentation including a three-minute product video, technical documentation, and reflective reports addressing the development process and team-related learning experiences. Feedback is provided through interim presentations, reflection sessions, teacher or coach feedback, and peer or stakeholder-informed discussion.

### Reflection Design

Reflection is embedded throughout the module, not added only at the end. Students reflect on teamwork, process decisions, failures, learning strategies, communication, uncertainty, and the societal responsibility of engineering. Student statements describe reflection on product and process as intensive and demanding, but helpful for understanding and justifying their own decisions.

## Phase-by-Phase Walkthrough

### Phase 1 – Engage

Students are introduced to the annual Engineers Without Borders Challenge, the partner context, and the broader framing of responsible engineering early on. They experience minor challenges, such as the Marshmallow Challenge, after group matching so that they can directly learn and reflect on teamwork.

### Phase 2 – Investigate

Students analyse challenge fact sheets, local constraints, user and stakeholder needs, sustainability dimensions, cultural practices, available resources, and feasibility requirements. Method labs introduce user-centred design, ideation and rapid prototyping. Teams identify assumptions, research missing information, prepare questions for feedback and engage in expert consultation.

### Phase 3 – Act / Prototype

Students work independently in teams over several weeks to develop, test and iterate solution concepts and functional prototypes. The process is deliberately open-ended: students handle uncertainty, negotiate decisions, make trade-offs between technical feasibility and contextual appropriateness, and adapt ideas after feedback.

### Phase 4 – Reflect & Present

The module concludes with final presentations including short videos of the developed concepts and prototypes, technical documentation, and reflective reports. Reflection addresses both the artefact and the learning process: decisions, collaboration, failures or changes, handling ambiguity, and responsible engineering practice.

## Instructor Perspective

### Preparation Required

Instructors need to secure the annual challenge fact sheets and partner context, prepare background materials, coordinate stakeholder or expert input and question-and-answer sessions, design method labs, create milestones and feedback formats, prepare rooms and prototyping resources, set up collaboration tools, and define assessment expectations. They also prepare scaffolding for ambiguity, team conflicts, time management, and reflective writing.

### Instructor Role During Module

The instructor role shifts from content expert to facilitator, coach, process designer, critical friend, and reflection moderator. The instructor does not prescribe a single correct solution but supports students in making justified decisions, testing assumptions, engaging with stakeholder perspectives, and learning from uncertainty and failure.

### Challenges & Tips

Key challenges named in the supplied account are ambiguity, uneven team motivation, organisational complexity, and the risk that students interpret the task as either too open or too technically constrained. The supplied account recommends early process scaffolding, visible milestones, method labs early in the semester, regular feedback loops, and explicit reflection on ambiguity.

### Variations / Adaptations

The format can be adapted across disciplines by changing the challenge topic, stakeholder constellation, prototype expectations, and depth of technical implementation. It has been used as a master module, bachelor project, interdisciplinary block format, or international collaborative challenge. In summer terms when the Engineers Without Borders challenge is on hold, lecturers develop challenges with external partners and colleagues on topics such as explainable AI, robotics, energy transition, and remote laboratories.
