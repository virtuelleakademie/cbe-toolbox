# Challenges & Opportunities in the Food System

> **Collection note.** This project-authored case preserves the supplied module account, its distinctions and its visible gaps. The supplied office or PDF file and the assessment workbook are not bibliographic sources.

## Summary

By conducting an in-depth analysis of the BFH’s catering system, students are able to propose practical measures tailored to the canteens and their customers, thereby helping to achieve the climate targets set by the BFH.

## Challenge & Stakeholders

### Challenge Topic

The campus catering system under the BFH’s 2030 Climate Roadmap

### Challenge Formulation

To analyse the campus catering system from multiple perspectives and propose strategic measures to support a transition of the current system toward achieving the BFH’s climate objectives.

### Challenge Authenticity / Origin

Instructor-designed in collaboration with the catering manager at BFH

### Challenge Owner / Stakeholders

Primary problem owner: BFH as an institution

Shared responsibility: catering service provider

Affected stakeholders:

- students

- BFH staff

- Catering staff

- food suppliers and producers

- BFH sustainibility and climate teams

### Stakeholder Involvement Moments

Presentation during the challenge (week 2), availability for interviews, feedback and questions during the final presentation

## Constructive Alignment

### Intended Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

Identify and explain the requirements and needs of different actors and stakeholders within a specific segment of the food system (BFH collective catering)

Analyze and critically examine relationships within a specific segment of the food system (BFH collective catering)

Identify and assess key levers and factors within the food system that have positive or negative impacts on the planet, society (including human health), and the economy

Analyze and explain linear and non-linear relationships and interdependencies between people, societal domains, and the natural environment at both local and global levels within the collective catering system

Manage system complexity, analyze root causes, and identify actionable levers to support the positive transformation of the current system

### Key Learning Activities

Research and data collection in various catering establishments within the BFH.

Excursion to the University of Lausanne and the EPFL canteen

### Assessment Criteria / Rubrics

- Quality of the analysis of the current situation and the need for change (Iceberg Model)

- Link to the theory of change

- Clarity of the vision for long-term positive change

- Accurate identification of stakeholders (mapping)

- Proposed activities/projects: contribution to realising the vision, structure, resources, effort-to-impact ratio

- Formulation of realistic and achievable outcomes

- Consideration and planning of the resources required to carry out the activities/projects

-Innovative dimension of the proposed activities/projects

- Capacity for analysis and critical thinking

### Assessment Evidence & Feedback

40%: individual implementation report.

60%: oral presentation of the theory of change for the selected challenge

### Reflection Design

Report & presentation

## Instructor Perspective

### Preparation Required

Partners and excursion

### Instructor Role During Module

The role of the instructor in this module is not the traditional “sage on the stage” (a lecture-based posture of the all-knowing teacher). Instead, the instructor adopts the role of a facilitator who is attentive to the needs and expectations of the students (“guide on the side”).

This innovative posture requires a high degree of flexibility: rather than imposing fixed content, the instructor continuously adjusts their approach based on the students’ interests, aspirations, and intrinsic motivations. This approach promotes more engaged and meaningful learning, aligned with the real needs of the learners.

### Challenges & Tips

Challenges for the instructors is mainly linked to the high adaptability needed from one bloc to the next. Instructors need to accept a certain uncertainty and trust the process (not controlling every single aspect of the modules). Transparent communication with students help to build trust and help them understand that this uncertainty is part of the process particularly in complex system transformation.

### Variations / Adaptations

This module is currently being carried out for another challenge of the food system (e.g. food education of children in the family).

## Outcomes & Transferability

### Student Experience

> Practice observation, not empirically grounded. The following account is retained from the supplied module template; no independent outcome evidence is added.

The students’ feedback highlights several key elements. They particularly appreciated working on a realistic scenario: the catering system of their school. The “learning journeys” (to EPFL’s collective restaurant, a pioneer in sustainability, and to other BFH canteens) were perceived as enriching for understanding the system.

The integration of the module within an interdisciplinary approach, with various opportunities for exchange and discussion with stakeholders, was considered highly valuable, providing stimulating reflections and useful perspectives to refine their analyses.

Finally, the freedom given to students to organize themselves in groups and explore the issues independently was appreciated. Although the process of deep immersion (Theory U) in the system may have seemed unusual and even frustrating at first, it ultimately enabled the development of relevant insights and a better understanding of system dynamics and was regarded very positively overall.

### Transferability

The concept is transferable to other disciplines. The key requirements are a systemic problem to address and committed stakeholders willing to invest time in working with students.
