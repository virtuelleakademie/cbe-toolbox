# The Challenge Based Learning Three-Phase Framework

## Summary

Challenge Based Learning (CBL) organizes the learning experience into three interconnected phases: **Engage**, **Investigate**, and **Act**. Each phase includes activities that prepare the learners to move to the next stage, and supporting the entire process is an ongoing process of documenting, reflecting, and sharing (CBL Guide 2016, p. 11). The framework explicitly allows for returning to earlier phases when needed: solution development "will most likely raise new Guiding Questions … and swing them back into the Investigation Phase" (CBL Guide 2016, p. 41).

## Phase 1: Engage

Through a process of Essential Questioning, the learners move from an abstract **Big Idea** to a concrete and actionable **Challenge** (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 11, 31).

### Big Idea
A broad concept that can be explored in multiple ways and is relevant to the learners and the larger community. Examples from the guide: *Community, Relationships, Creativity, Health, Sustainability, Democracy* (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 11, 31–32).

### Essential Question
Essential Questioning allows the learners to contextualize and personalize the Big Idea by asking personal and contextual questions (e.g., *"Why is this important to me? Where does this concept intersect with my world?"*). The end product is a single Essential Question that is relevant to the individual or group (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12, 32–33).

### Challenge Statement
The Challenge turns the Essential Question into a **call to action** by charging participants to learn about the subject and develop a solution. Challenges are immediate and actionable (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12, 33–34). The Engage phase ends when the learners have identified a compelling and actionable Challenge statement — at which point the guide advises slowing the process down rather than jumping to solutions (CBL Guide 2016, p. 35).

## Phase 2: Investigate

All learners plan and participate in a journey that builds the foundation for solutions and addresses academic requirements (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12, 36).

### Guiding Questions
The phase begins by generating questions related to the Challenge — everything that needs to be learned to develop an informed solution. The learners consolidate similar questions, then categorize and prioritize them, producing a set of categories that contain prioritized questions — the research agenda (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12, 36–38).

### Guiding Activities & Resources
Learners identify resources (online databases and journals, online courses, libraries, social networks, experts) and activities (simulations, experiments, projects, problem sets, research, expert interviews, surveys, lectures) to answer the Guiding Questions (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12, 38).

### Analysis
Once the Guiding Questions have been addressed, learners analyze the accumulated data, identify themes, and move from a list of findings to research conclusions. The phase concludes with reports and presentations demonstrating that all Guiding Questions have been successfully answered, curriculum standards addressed, and clear conclusions developed that set the foundation for the solution (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12, 39–40).

## Phase 3: Act

Evidence-based solutions are developed, implemented with an authentic audience, and then evaluated based on the results (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 12–13, 40–41).

### Solution Concepts
Solution concepts emerge from the findings made during the Investigation phase: campaigns to inform or educate, school or community improvement projects, product development, or other activities (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 13, 41).

### Solution Development
Learners develop prototypes, experiment, and test. This iterative design cycle often surfaces new Guiding Questions, potentially pulling learners back into the Investigate phase (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 13, 41).

### Implementation & Evaluation
Implementation takes place within a real setting with an authentic audience. Learners measure outcomes, reflect on what worked and what didn't, and determine their impact on the Challenge; the work culminates in further refinement of the solution or a completion report shared with the rest of the world (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 13, 41–42).

## Cross-Cutting Process: Document, Reflect and Share

Throughout all phases, learners document their experience using audio, video, images, and photography. This ongoing collection (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 11, 42–43; also p. 10):
- Supports informative (formative) assessment
- Provides the resources for reflection
- Documents the learning process and tells the story of the Challenge

## Notes on Meta-Skills

> **Corpus annotation (2026-07-09):** The guide does **not** use the term "meta-skills" (nor "durable skills"). Its outcome term is **"21st-century skills"**, which learners "organically develop" through authentic learning experiences (CBL Guide 2016, pp. 4, 9). The closest analogue to a meta-skill focus is the cross-cutting *Document, Reflect and Share* process (pp. 42–43) and the Welcome text's goal of "developing a framework for life-long learning" (p. 4). This is an implicit, not explicit, meta-skill framing. See `vocabulary/vocabulary-001-cbl-core-vocabulary.md` (vocabulary-001) for the full terminology discussion.
