# The PIONEER Ten-Step CBE Module Design Flow

> **Provenance.** Grounded in the validated PIONEER Working Package 4 source
> *4.2.1 Sub-Task Didactical Toolbox — Train-the-Trainer*, §4.6 ("Design of CBE
> Module — Step-by-Step Guide"). This is designer-time process guidance for
> structuring the work of designing a CBE module. It does **not** replace the
> Challenge Based Learning three-phase design spine documented in
> `methodology-001`, nor the PIONEER CBE definition (`foundations-005`) or the
> maturity continuum (`methodology-007`) it is designed alongside.

## Summary

PIONEER WP4 offers a **ten-step design flow** to help a development team structure the process of designing (or redesigning) a CBE module. The flow is built on a **backward-design** logic: the intended learning outcomes and the assessment evidence are settled *before* the challenge itself is detailed (evidence first). After the assessment step it inserts an explicit **coherence gate** — a review moment where the team confirms that challenge, learning outcomes, and assessment blueprint hang together before continuing. Crucially, §4.6 recommends using the flow **iteratively rather than linearly**, in approximately three rounds (rough → refined → detailed). Each step produces a named **output artefact**, so the flow doubles as a checklist of what a complete design contains.

## Iterative use — three rounds

§4.6 opens with its own usage recommendation, and it is part of the method, not an editorial gloss: *the CBE design flow is helpful to structure the process, however it is recommended to be used iteratively rather than linearly.* PIONEER suggests **working in approximately three rounds — rough design, refined design, and detailed design** — which makes the flow more realistic and reduces rework later.

The ten steps below therefore are **not a mandatory linear sequence**. They are an ordering of concerns to revisit across rounds: a first pass sets rough shapes for all ten, a second pass refines them once their interactions are visible, and a third pass fills in detail. Steps are interlinked (step 2 is "interlinked with other steps"; step 6's alignment "requires flexibility"), so later steps routinely send the team back to revise earlier ones.

## Backward design and the coherence gate

The flow front-loads the **why** and the **evidence**. Before the challenge is designed in detail, the team defines *what students should learn* (step 2) and decides *how learning will be assessed* (step 3) — **evidence first**: for each learning outcome the team names the evidence that will prove it, and what is graded (summative) versus practised (formative). Only then is the challenge itself developed further (step 4).

Because assessment is one of the pain points of the design process, §4.6 adds an **explicit review moment after step 3** — a **coherence gate**: *do not move on before you make sure that the initial challenge, learning outcomes and assessment blueprint are coherent.* This gate is the flow's constructive-alignment checkpoint at design time; step 9 later re-runs a fuller alignment check across all activities and deliverables.

## The ten steps

Each step below carries its intent and, verbatim from §4.6, its named **Output** artefact.

### Step 1 — Set the frame: what are your constraints

Establish a shared understanding of what a "good challenge" looks like and how interdisciplinarity and interculturality will be handled, and get the big picture of the challenge clear (theme, issue, stakeholder) before defining learning outcomes. Discuss the development team's educational visions, since they may differ. Define the "playing field": level (BA/MA/PhD); ECTS/workload, duration, class size; delivery mode (offline / online / hybrid); resources and institutional rules; and the scope of the module (what is in and what is out). Clarify team roles, responsibilities and decision-making, and confirm the time and budget available for development.

**Output:** Module meta information (level, ECTS, delivery mode, etc.).

### Step 2 — Define what students should learn

An essential step, interlinked with the others. Define the intended learning outcomes with a competence orientation, focusing on observable performance rather than only "know/understand".

**Output:** Learning Outcomes list.

### Step 3 — Decide how you will assess learning (evidence first)

For each learning outcome decide: what evidence will prove it (product + process), and what is graded (summative) versus practised with feedback (formative). Assessment is one of the pain points in the design process, so an explicit review moment is recommended after this step — the **coherence gate** above: do not move on before the initial challenge, learning outcomes and assessment blueprint are coherent.

**Output:** Assessment Blueprint (plus rubric skeleton).

### Step 4 — Choose the challenge

Develop the "big picture" of the initial challenge further and "design" a challenge that is feasible in the timeframe. Define it so it is real, open-ended and meaningful — the real-world anchor — with the societal contribution made explicit (SDG11: inclusive, safe, resilient, sustainable cities), interdisciplinary, and feasible within the constraints. Use this ladder: **theme/big idea → key question → concrete challenge**.

**Output:** Challenge statement (draft).

### Step 5 — Plan stakeholder involvement

Clarify the details of stakeholder involvement: who is the challenge owner/stakeholder; what value/deliverable they need; and when they will interact with students (briefing, feedback, final). Define touchpoints such as a kick-off briefing, a mid-point review, and a final pitch/jury.

**Output:** Stakeholder & engagement plan.

### Step 6 — Plan the learning journey

Create a simple timeline with phases and checkpoints, using the time and resources available and considering the semester timeline, international collaboration and the stakeholder plan. The alignment can be challenging and requires flexibility and/or unconventional solutions. The learning journey has **four elements**:

- **Engage** — understand the topic, define/refine the challenge, set direction.
- **Investigate** — research, collect evidence, analyse, test assumptions.
- **Act/prototype** — develop the solution, prototype/implement (as feasible), evaluate impact.
- **Reflect and present.**

**Output:** Phase plan / module timeline.

### Step 7 — Design learning activities

Choose and design the activities that make students practise, develop and design what will be assessed, coordinating interdisciplinary and/or international student groups and the stakeholder activities: activities per phase (Engage / Investigate / Act–prototype / Reflect–present); teamwork structure and facilitation moments; tool use (especially online/hybrid); and the didactical methods to be used.

**Output:** Learning Activity Plan (sessions/weeks + key didactical methods).

### Step 8 — Define deliverables

A refinement of the deliverables discussed in the "big picture". Define what students submit/produce and when, and list the concrete products along the way: early (challenge brief / guiding questions / research plan), mid (research synthesis / insights / prototype drafts), and final (solution + evaluation + presentation + reflection). Link each deliverable to the assessment plan.

**Output:** Deliverables map (deliverable → timing → assessed/not assessed).

### Step 9 — Run an alignment check

Check the "triangle" / constructive alignment: every learning outcome is trained in activities; every learning outcome is assessed with evidence; and no big graded item appears that students did not practise for.

**Output:** Alignment matrix + fixes.

### Step 10 — Prepare to run it

Prepare to run it (facilitation + logistics). Logistics, communication and handover are often underestimated even though they strongly affect implementation quality. Confirm the elements of the module: roles (lecturer as facilitator/coach), checkpoints, support for teams; stakeholder availability; tools/rooms and accessibility; and data/ethics compliance.

**Output:** Delivery & readiness checklist.

## Designer-time flow vs learner-time journey

The ten-step flow and the CBL design spine (`methodology-001`) share a **structural correspondence** but operate on **different axes** — the two do not replace each other.

- **Step 4's ladder — theme/big idea → key question → concrete challenge — is designer-time.** It mirrors the CBL Engage substructure (Big Idea → Essential Question → Challenge Statement) as a *design move*: it is how the design team narrows an abstract theme down to a concrete challenge statement while designing the module. It is process guidance for the designer.
- **Step 6's four-element learning journey — Engage / Investigate / Act–prototype / Reflect and present — is learner-time.** Its first three elements match `methodology-001`'s learner-time phases (Engage, Investigate, Act); the fourth, *Reflect and present*, is authoritative in the final 4.2.1. This axis describes what **students** move through during the module.

So the design flow is the axis along which a lecturer *builds* the module; the learner journey is the axis along which *students* live it. A lecturer can be at step 6 of the design flow (planning the whole learner journey) in a single designer-time sitting. Keeping the two axes distinct prevents the common confusion of reading the ten design steps as ten things students do, or the four learner-journey elements as a design checklist.
