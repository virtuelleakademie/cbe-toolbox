# AI Delegation and Cognitive Load: Germane vs Extraneous Processing

## Summary

Cognitive Load Theory distinguishes germane processing — the working-memory work that builds the targeted schemas, which must happen in the learner's head — from extraneous processing, which can be delegated to AI or tools without harming the learning yield.

## The distinction that makes delegation answerable

The question "should students use generative AI for this?" has no defensible answer
until the design knows *what cognitive work constructs the targeted competence*.
Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller) supplies the discriminator:

- **Germane load** — the effortful processing that constructs and automates the
  schemas the module is trying to teach. This is the learning. Delegating it
  removes the learning.
- **Extraneous load** — processing imposed by the form of the task rather than its
  learning substance: formatting, boilerplate, search overhead, mechanical
  transformation. Delegating it frees working-memory capacity for germane work.
- **Intrinsic load** — the inherent difficulty of the material itself, set by
  element interactivity. Not delegable, but manageable through sequencing.

The Delegation Check applies this per **challenge component**, not to the module as
a whole: a single CBE challenge mixes components whose load profile differs.

## Three delegation statuses, not two

The germane/extraneous distinction is a spectrum, and a two-way choice — keep it or
give it away — forces components into the wrong box. A component is placed in one of
three statuses:

- **Student-generated.** The cognitive work is germane; the student produces it
  without generative AI. Anything the assessment claims to evidence belongs here.
- **Hybrid.** The student produces the work, and AI acts on it afterwards as a
  **critic** (challenging the draft, surfacing gaps, arguing the other side) or
  beforehand as a **scaffold** (worked examples, structure, vocabulary) — never as
  the author of the artefact. This is where most contested components belong: the
  germane work stays with the learner while the extraneous friction around it drops.
- **Delegable.** The work is extraneous to the targeted competence and can be handed
  to AI outright.

A component argued into Hybrid needs the same justification as one argued into either
extreme: *which* part is the student's and *what* the AI does with it.

## Per-component heuristic

For each component a lecturer names, ask: *does the cognitive work of this component
construct the competence the module targets (germane → keep in the student's head),
or is it overhead around that competence (extraneous → delegable)?*

| Component pattern | Likely load | Delegation default (lecturer to justify) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generating the core argument / analysis / design decision | germane | not delegable |
| Synthesising sources into the learner's own framing | germane | not delegable |
| Formatting, citation cleanup, layout | extraneous | delegable |
| Boilerplate scaffolding (templates, skeletons) | extraneous | delegable |
| First-draft translation / transcription | mixed — depends on whether language production is a target | lecturer decides per goal |

The table is a starting point for the lecturer's reasoning, never a verdict the
skill issues on its own. The skill elicits a per-component decision **with the
lecturer's justification**, records the reasoning, and evaluates whether the
reasoning is consistent with the germane/extraneous distinction — it never authors
the decision.

## Ownership is the load a delegation argument most often misses

Some components carry germane load of a particular kind: they are what makes the
challenge the students' own. The Big Idea they settle on, the Essential Question they
formulate, the reflection in which they judge their own work — these carry the
**ownership** that licenses everything downstream. A challenge the students did not
frame is an assignment they were handed.

Ownership does not survive being generated for them. Where a component of that kind
is argued as delegable, the argument has usually weighed the *output* (a question is
a sentence; AI writes good sentences) and not the *work* (formulating the question is
where the students decide what they care about). Name which ownership is at stake,
say what collapses without it, and look for the hybrid form: the students formulate,
the AI challenges what they formulated.

This is a reason to examine an argument, never a reason to overrule a decision. A
lecturer may have grounds a general heuristic cannot see — a cohort that has already
done this work, a module where the question is given by an external partner.

## Why the upstream checks are prerequisites

"Germane" has no referent until the Competence Check has established *which*
competence is targeted and the Alignment Check has established *how* it is assessed.
Running the Delegation Check first reduces "germane vs extraneous" to aesthetic
preference. This is the structural reason for the Competence → Alignment →
Delegation ordering.
